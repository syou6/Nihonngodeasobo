You studied for years. Your grammar is solid, your vocabulary is wide — and yet, the moment you open your mouth, people hear that you're not a native speaker. The reason usually isn't your grammar or even your pronunciation. It's pitch accent.

English stresses. Japanese pitches.

English is a stress-accent language: we make one syllable louder and longer (a-MA-zing). Japanese is a pitch-accent language: every mora is roughly the same length and loudness, but the pitch jumps high or drops low at specific points. When you carry your English stress habit into Japanese, you flatten the pitch or put the drop in the wrong place — and that mismatch is exactly what makes you "sound foreign."

It changes meaning, not just accent

Pitch isn't cosmetic. Same sounds + different pitch = different word:

Why it's so hard to fix alone

Here's the trap: you can't hear your own pitch errors. Your brain already "knows" what you meant, so it papers over the wrong melody. Natives rarely correct you (it's awkward), and recording yourself doesn't help if you can't tell where the pitch went wrong. The feedback loop stays open — so the error never gets fixed, no matter how many hours you study.

How to actually fix it

  1. Hear the drop. Train your ear on minimal pairs until you can hear where the pitch falls.
  2. Produce it. Say the word and compare your pitch contour to a native one — visually, not by feel.
  3. Get honest feedback. A tool that detects your actual pitch and tells you "you kept it flat — the drop should land after し" closes the loop your own ears can't.
